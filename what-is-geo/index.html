What Is Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)?

Understand how AI-powered search systems discover, interpret and cite information, and what businesses can do to be represented accurately in AI-generated answers.

Written by Tandeep Sangra · Last updated 17 October 2026

TL;DR — GEO = Generative Engine Optimization. It means optimizing a business's website, content, and online presence so that AI tools such as ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews, Gemini, and Perplexity are more likely to understand, mention, cite, recommend, or correctly represent the business in their answers. This guide explains how it works, what the research shows (and does not show), and how to measure it.

Ask an AI tool "who is the best provider of this in my situation?" and it does not hand you ten links. It writes a recommendation. It names two or three businesses, says what each is good for, and sometimes explains why it left others out. For many buyers, that shortlist is now the first and sometimes the only research step, and it happens before any website is opened.

That changes what it means to be found. Your business is not competing for one position on one page any more. It is competing to be understood, trusted and described correctly across every question a buyer might ask: who is best, how do these two compare, is this company any good. If the AI cannot find reliable information about you, or finds conflicting versions of it, you are left off the list or described wrongly, and you never learn that the conversation took place. GEO is the practice of dealing with exactly that.

What Is Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)?

Definition

GEO = Generative Engine Optimization. It means optimizing a business's website, content, and online presence so that AI tools such as ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews, Gemini, and Perplexity are more likely to understand, mention, cite, recommend, or correctly represent the business in their answers.

"Generative" simply means the AI writes a fresh answer instead of only listing links. That is true of ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Microsoft Copilot, Claude and the AI answers inside Google. So GEO is less about one page ranking for one keyword, and more about the overall picture of your business that an AI builds from everything it can find about you.

Why GEO is more than "optimizing for an answer"

Winning one direct answer to one question is a narrower job, and we cover it separately as AEO. GEO looks at your whole presence, because buyers ask AI three different kinds of questions, and each needs a different kind of proof:

Shortlist questions

"Who are the best…?"

The AI picks a few names. Being on the list depends on whether it understands what category you are in and who you serve.

Comparison questions

"How does A compare with B?"

The AI weighs you against a rival using whatever facts it can find. Vague or missing details hand the advantage to the competitor with clearer ones.

Reputation questions

"Is this company any good?"

The AI leans on what others say about you, such as reviews, press and profiles, not only on your own site.

You may also see this work called AI search optimization, LLM optimization or AI SEO. The names vary; the aim is the same. Our AI SEO glossary and LLM optimization guide cover the wording.

What does GEO mean for a business?

Imagine someone asks an AI assistant: "Which project management software is best for a distributed team?" or "What should I look for when choosing a cybersecurity consultancy?" The AI may write a synthesized answer, mention several businesses, compare them and link to its sources. For your business to be represented well, five things need to come together:

01DiscoverabilitySystems can reach your information.
02ClarityYour site says what you do, for whom, and why you differ.
03EvidenceClaims are backed by data, examples or references.
04ConsistencyNames, services and credentials agree across sources.
05RelevanceYour content answers the question actually asked.

GEO is not about inserting more keywords or publishing more articles. It is about making relevant, trustworthy information easier to find, interpret, evaluate and use.

Four different outcomes. Being understood, being mentioned, being cited and being recommended are not the same thing. A business can be mentioned without a citation, cited without a recommendation, or described accurately without anyone visiting its website.

Why Is GEO Becoming Important?

Traditional search gives users a list of results to explore. Generative search can synthesize an answer first, so people understand a topic before deciding which sources or businesses to look at. A business can have a good website and still be missing from a particular AI answer, and it can be discussed in an answer even though the user never visits its site. Three developments make GEO worth understanding:

1 · Discovery

Answers are synthesized

AI tools combine several sources and link to supporting pages. The opportunity is no longer only to appear in a list, but to be represented in the answer. Venture firm a16z noted that AI queries average about 23 words against roughly 4 for traditional search, so people describe their situation in detail.

2 · Quality

Substance beats keywords

A page can repeat a keyword without showing real expertise. Specific definitions, verifiable claims, original examples and clear explanations give readers, and the systems retrieving the page, more to work with. Google's guidance likewise stresses original, useful content.

3 · Measurement

A mention is not a result

A mention is not an endorsement, a citation does not prove the AI got it right, and appearing in an answer does not automatically create a lead. So the questions are whether you appear, and whether what is said is correct and useful.

GEO gives businesses a way to examine these outcomes systematically, while accepting that no technique guarantees inclusion.

How Do Generative Engines Work?

Generative engines do not all work the same way. Some answers draw on what a model learned during training; others use web search or retrieval to fetch current information first; some combine both. Google has publicly described its AI search features as using retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and "query fan-out", where related searches are run to gather information for one question. Other platforms have their own methods, so do not assume one universal formula.

From question to answer: a simplified model

This is a conceptual picture, not a claim that every platform follows these exact steps.

  1. The system interprets the questionA request like "best CRM for a small B2B SaaS company" holds several needs: software choice, company size, industry and likely requirements such as integrations. For businesses: write for real customer questions and decision criteria, not only short generic keywords.
  2. It finds informationDepending on the platform and query, it may use learned knowledge, search the web, retrieve indexed pages, or combine these. A page that is inaccessible cannot support an answer, though being accessible does not guarantee it will be chosen. For businesses: keep key content accessible and technically sound.
  3. It weighs the sourcesRelevance, reliable evidence, clear explanation and corroboration may help a source contribute, but the exact process varies. Independent reporting and specialist publications can shape how a business is represented too. For businesses: make claims verifiable and keep your core facts consistent.
  4. It writes the answerThe model may blend several documents into a new response, which can also introduce omissions or errors. For businesses: explain concepts in context so a claim is not easily misread on its own.
  5. It shows the resultThe answer may name a business, cite its site, recommend a product, or leave it out. A citation shows a source was attributed; it does not mean every claim is correct or that you were recommended. For businesses: judge the accuracy of how you are represented, not only how often.

What Does GEO Research Actually Show?

Here is what has been studied, what was found, and how much confidence to place in it.

Peer-reviewed · ACM SIGKDD 2024

The study that introduced GEO

Pranjal Aggarwal and co-authors introduced GEO as a way to study how content creators can improve visibility in generative engine responses. They built GEO-bench, a benchmark of 10,000 queries across varied topics, and tested strategies such as adding statistics, citations and quotations, and improving fluency. They reported visibility improvements of up to 40% under their experimental conditions, and found that effectiveness varied by domain.

What 40% means: an experimental result about a source's representation in generated answers. It does not show that any website will get 40% more mentions, traffic, leads or revenue. Read the paper →
arXiv preprint · 2025

AI search can favour different sources

Mahe Chen, Xiaoxuan Wang, Kaiwen Chen and Nick Koudas compared AI search with traditional search. They reported that AI search tools relied heavily on earned media, meaning third-party authoritative sources, and gave far less weight to brand-owned and social content, and that engines differed in the number of sites they cite, source freshness and sensitivity to rephrased queries.

Why it matters: your own site must be clear, but credible outside sources may shape how you are described. This is no reason to chase artificial mentions. It is a preprint, so read it in light of its design. Read the preprint →
arXiv preprint · 2026 critical survey

What the wider evidence says, and what is uncertain

Olivier Martinez reviewed 45 GEO studies published between November 2023 and July 2026. The survey concludes that gains reported in the original paper hold within its experimental setting, where a source is already in the model's context, but they do not show lasting organic discoverability or traffic effects. Topical relevance and where information sits in the context were the most reproducible factors; generic tactics transferred poorly; and audits of commercial engines showed low source overlap and substantial run-to-run variability. It also warns that some citation-oriented rewrites can hurt retrieval.

Takeaway: GEO is an emerging discipline, not a settled formula. Apply sound content and technical principles, test the results, and treat guaranteed tactics with suspicion. Read the survey →

What can a business reasonably conclude?

  • Relevant, evidence-based content is a sensible foundation.
  • Content changes may influence how information is used in answers, depending on the setting.
  • Outside sources and brand reputation can matter alongside your own website.
  • AI visibility varies across systems, queries and time.
  • No single checklist guarantees mentions, citations, recommendations or results.

What GEO Means for Your Website, Content and Online Presence

1 · Website clarity and accessibility

Make it easy to tell what you do, who you serve, what you offer, what sets it apart, who is responsible for the expertise shown, and where the evidence is. Good technical SEO, crawlability, sensible navigation, internal links and readable text form the base. Google states that existing SEO practice stays relevant for its AI search, and that meeting technical requirements does not guarantee inclusion.

2 · Content that answers real questions

A page should explain a subject, not just mention it. "Our platform offers enterprise-grade security" tells a reader little; naming the controls, scope, certifications, limits and documentation tells them a lot. Use direct explanations, attributed statistics, original examples and honest comparisons. These are principles for useful, verifiable content, not guaranteed ranking signals.

3 · A credible, consistent online presence

Information about you also comes from publications, documentation, reviews, professional profiles and industry resources. If your site calls a product an analytics platform while public profiles call it a support tool, your positioning is harder to interpret. Keep names, descriptions and credentials current and aligned, and correct inaccurate public information. The aim is accuracy, never manufactured coverage.

4 · Structure that is easy to interpret

Clear headings, descriptive titles, meaningful links, concise definitions and comparison tables help readers and systems. A pricing page should say what each plan includes and its limits; an expert article should name its author and cite evidence. Structured data can help search engines interpret eligible content, but it is not a special GEO switch.

Practical Examples of GEO

Three fictional examples from different kinds of business. They are illustrative, and any improved wording should be used only when it is true.

Example 1 · A software product page

AcmeFlow is a made-up project management platform.

Before

"AcmeFlow is an innovative, industry-leading platform that transforms productivity for modern businesses."

Problem: generic language, vague claims, nothing verifiable.

After

"AcmeFlow is a project management platform for distributed teams. It provides task assignment, project timelines and progress reporting in one workspace, and is designed for teams that coordinate work across departments."

Why it is better: category, audience and core capabilities are explicit.

Example 2 · A local clinic with inconsistent details

Brightside Dental is a made-up clinic. A patient asks an AI: "Which dentist near me does Invisalign and is open on Saturdays?"

Before

The website says "the best dental care in town". Google and a directory list different opening hours, one profile still shows an old address, and no page says which treatments the clinic offers or who its dentists are.

Problem: the AI finds conflicting facts and no clear answer, so it may skip the clinic or repeat the wrong hours.

After

One treatments page lists Invisalign, whitening and implants. The dentists' names and qualifications are published. Address, phone and opening hours, including Saturdays, match on the website and every profile.

Why it is better: the facts agree everywhere, so the AI has something reliable to repeat.

Example 3 · An online store that wants to be compared fairly

Calmleaf is a made-up skincare brand. A shopper asks: "What is a good fragrance-free moisturiser for dry, sensitive skin?"

Before

"Our gentle, luxurious moisturiser is loved by everyone."

Problem: it says nothing the AI can match to the shopper's need, so a rival with clearer product detail gets named instead.

After

"Calmleaf Barrier Cream is a fragrance-free moisturiser for dry and sensitive skin. The full ingredient list, skin-type guidance and what it is not suited for (such as very oily skin) are on the product page, with independent customer reviews."

Why it is better: the product answers the shopper's exact question, openly and with outside proof.

None of these rewrites is guaranteed to be cited or recommended. They simply give people and systems something clearer to work with. The same applies to proof: if a company says customers save 30% of their time, it should point to a real study, explain how it was measured, and not present it as a universal result.

GEO vs SEO vs AEO

SEO, answer engine optimization (AEO) and GEO are overlapping approaches to improving how information is found, understood and presented in search and AI experiences.

Primary focusExample
SEOHelping web pages be discoverable and perform in searchTechnical accessibility, content relevance, internal links
AEOGiving useful, direct answers in answer-focused experiencesClearly answering a common customer question
GEOImproving the chance useful information is included, cited or represented in generated responsesPublishing an evidence-backed comparison that explains a product category

These are practical distinctions, not universally agreed boundaries, and practitioners use AEO and GEO in overlapping ways. Google itself describes optimizing for its generative AI search as an extension of SEO, not a separate system. So GEO does not replace SEO. How Do You Measure GEO?

Asking an AI one question and checking for your name is not reliable, because answers change with wording, platform, context and time. A more meaningful approach tracks five things:

1

Brand mentions

Do systems name you for relevant questions? Track across a defined set of prompts and platforms.

2

Citations

Which of your pages are cited, and in what context?

3

Accuracy

Are your products, services, expertise and limits described correctly?

4

Share of recommendations

When providers are compared, are you included where you genuinely fit? Compare against the same prompts and competitors.

5

Traffic and outcomes

Where measurable, look at referral visits, enquiries and conversions. A mention alone does not prove impact.

  1. Set a baselineChoose questions relevant to your business.
  2. Test across platformsRun them on the AI tools you care about, and record the date.
  3. Record what you seeMentions, citations, accuracy and competing sources.
  4. Make documented changesContent or technical, one clear change at a time where possible.
  5. Repeat over timeRe-run the same tests, allowing for natural variation in answers.
  6. Compare and investigateDo not credit one change for every difference; observed change is not proven cause.

Common GEO Myths

"GEO guarantees AI will recommend you."

No. Inclusion depends on the platform, query and available sources.

"GEO replaces SEO."

No. Established SEO practice remains relevant, and GEO adds attention to citation and representation.

"Schema markup guarantees AI citations."

No. Google says no special schema is required for its generative AI features. Markup should simply be accurate and match the visible content.

"More content means more visibility."

No. Repetitive or thin pages do not show expertise; original, useful information does.

"A citation means the AI endorses you."

No. A citation identifies a source; it does not imply agreement or a recommendation.

"One technique works on every platform."

No. Platforms retrieve and select sources differently, and results vary by topic and conditions. There is no proven universal formula.

The Future of GEO: Be Useful, Understandable and Trustworthy

GEO is still evolving. Research so far suggests content quality and presentation can influence how generative systems use information, and it also shows why universal promises deserve caution: visibility varies across platforms, queries and settings, and the link between optimization and business results is not fully established.

A practical starting point is simple: make your website accessible, explain your products and services clearly, support important claims with credible evidence, keep information accurate across relevant sources, and check how AI systems describe you over time. The goal is not just to appear more often, but to make sure that when you are relevant to a question, what is available about you gives people, and the systems helping them, a clear and accurate picture.
